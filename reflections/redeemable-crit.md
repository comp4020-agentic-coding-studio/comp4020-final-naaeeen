# Giving a room a reason to return to

A friend could visit someone else's bedroom, use the desk, and open their own
unrelated work card. That mismatch made the problem with the room clearer: a
personal-looking space needs something that makes the visit about its owner.

I chose one owner-selected reference image with a saved inspection view. It can
be a diagram, design or illustration rather than a compulsory work report. The
owner can return to the same material; a visitor has something specific to look
at and discuss. Another timer would do little for a visit, while general media
would bring more formats and playback decisions before resolving this basic task.

[Harrison and Dourish's author-hosted draft](https://dourish.com/~dourishc/publications/1996/cscw96-place.pdf)
distinguishes geometric space from socially understood place. I take that as a
design question: what would friends expect to find here? Saving the owner's
reference gives the desk a more coherent answer. The reference connects the owner,
the object and what a visitor expects to find.

The interesting boundary is between sharing a subject and sharing control.
Visitors can pan and zoom without overwriting the owner's saved view. They can
look at different details while remaining in the same room. The cost is that friends
cannot assume they see the same detail: they need to say which part they mean.
Tests check the separate views and door permissions.

There are costs: the owner must bring content, PNG limits expression, and a room
adds navigation compared with chat. If the visit only adds walking, the design
needs changing. I want to become a developer who can explain why an interaction
belongs somewhere, rather than treating a furnished space as enough reason to
use it.
